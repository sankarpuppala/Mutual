# Taking part in the Mutual study — information for participants

Status: **DRAFT, for the ethics application.** Nobody may be shown this as a
real invitation until written approval exists and `study/APPROVAL.md` records
it.

Written for students who are being invited to take part — not for the
researcher, and not for the committee. Plain words, short sentences, nothing
you need a background in computing to follow.

---

## What is this?

Mutual is an app built by a student as a research project. It notices, over
Bluetooth, which other students you spent a little time near during the day,
and the next morning it shows you those people — through their written
answers, not their photos. You can send them a private signal for one of five
reasons: study, project, fitness, friend or date. **If they do not send one
back for the same reason, nothing happens, and nobody ever learns you sent
it** — not them, and not the people running the app.

The study is about **when photos should appear**. For two weeks the app will
show photos as soon as two people match. For the next two weeks it will show
them only after the two of you have written a few messages to each other. Then
it switches back, and back again. The question is whether that timing changes
anything: who gets matches, how conversations go, and whether people find it
frustrating.

## Why are you being asked?

Because you are a student at this college, over 18, and you might find the app
useful. There is no other reason, and nothing about you was looked at to
choose you.

## Do you have to take part?

No. Taking part is your choice, and nobody will know whether you did or not.

There is one thing to be clear about: while the study is running on this
campus, the photo-timing setting changes for **everyone** using the app here —
it is not possible to use the app with one setting while other people use
another. So during the study, using Mutual and taking part in the study are the
same thing. If you would rather not take part, the honest answer is to not use
the app during the study, and to come back afterwards.

## What happens if you take part?

You use the app normally. That is all. There is nothing extra to do, no diary
to keep, and no sessions to attend.

At the end of each fortnight the app will ask you **three optional questions**,
each a tap on a five-point scale:

- whether you felt comfortable using it;
- whether not seeing photos was frustrating;
- whether you were satisfied overall.

You can skip them.

## What information is collected about you?

**Counts, and nothing else.** Specifically, per week:

- whether you opened the app;
- how many matches you had;
- how many messages you sent;
- how many conversations you ended;
- which photo-timing setting was active;
- your answers to those three questions, if you give them.

Your identity is replaced by a code before any of it is analysed, and the key
that links the code to you is destroyed when the analysis is finished.

## What is **never** collected?

- Your name, your answers, your photos, your voice notes, or any message you
  write.
- Who you signalled, or whether you signalled anyone at all. The app is built
  so that this cannot be known even by the people running it.
- Who you crossed paths with. None of that leaves the study aside, and it is
  never part of the research data.
- Where you have been. The app never uses GPS or any location permission —
  there is not even one to grant.
- Your email address or your date of birth. The app checks your age once and
  then throws the date away.

## What are the risks?

**Being recognised.** Your first name, your year and your answers are visible
to people you cross paths with on campus. That is how the app works, and it is
the main thing to think about before joining.

**An awkward match.** A mutual match means you both signalled each other. You
can block or unmatch at any moment, and blocking is silent — the other person
is not told.

**One thing to know about ending a match.** If you unmatch or block someone,
the conversation is deleted for both of you, but they will still see that the
match happened and that it ended. Neither of you can remove a match from the
other person's history. That is deliberate: it stops anyone rewriting what
happened for someone else.

**Messages are not secret from the server.** Chat inside a match is not
end-to-end encrypted, so whoever runs the app's server can read messages inside
your matches. This is a prototype built by a student; it is being said plainly
rather than buried.

**Battery.** The app watches for nearby phones during the day, which uses some
battery. You can pause it at any time from the You tab.

## What are the benefits?

Possibly none to you personally. You may meet people you would otherwise only
have walked past. The research benefit is understanding whether a calmer,
photo-later design is workable — which is a question the whole field mostly
answers by assumption.

## Can you change your mind?

Yes, at any time, for any reason, without explaining.

- **Stop using the app:** just stop. Or pause discovery in the You tab.
- **Withdraw from the study:** there is a withdraw option in the app's
  settings. Your study data is deleted. Your account stays unless you delete it
  too.
- **Delete your account:** also in settings. Your profile, matches and messages
  are removed within 24 hours.

Withdrawing after the analysis has been published cannot un-publish it, but
your data can still be deleted.

## Who is doing this?

A student researcher, as part of a university project, supervised by an
academic member of staff. The contact details are at the bottom of this page.

## Who has approved it?

The ethics body named at the bottom of this page, under the reference given
there. If either is blank, **the study has not been approved and must not be
running.**

## Is the app secure?

It is an **unaudited research prototype**. The privacy design has been tested
by the people who built it, and the tests are public, but nobody independent
has audited it. It should not be treated as a finished, hardened product.

The one limitation worth stating in plain words: the design protects you from
someone *reading* the app's database. It does not protect you from someone who
*controls the server and actively tampers with it* — that person could learn
who you signalled. The project's own documentation says so
(`docs/THREAT_MODEL.md`).

## What happens to the results?

They are written up as part of a student project, and may be published. Only
counts and summaries appear — never anything that identifies a person, and
never a quotation from anyone's profile or messages. Null and negative results
are reported the same as positive ones.
